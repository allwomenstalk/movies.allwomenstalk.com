# 7 Key Differences Between Popular Books and Their Film Adaptations

When comparing popular books and their film adaptations, it is almost impossible not to spot the changes made when transitioning from the page to the big screen. As a book aficionado, it's sometimes hard to ignore any changes, minor or major, made in the final cut of a movie. When these alterations lean toward the drastic side, they can either hurt the film or help distinguish it as a standalone masterpiece. Exploring these key differences between books and movies gives us a fresh appreciation for storytelling in both mediums. Keeping this major spoiler warning in mind, here are seven famous books and their film adaptations known for their vast changes.

| Book & Film Title | Main Change | Impact on Adaptation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The Hunger Games | Omission of Madge & mockingjay pin origin | Alters Katniss's personal symbolism |
| Divergent | Toned-down attack scene by Al | Softens dark story undertones |
| The Lord of the Rings | Omission of Glorfindel & Tom Bombadil | Streamlines narrative pacing |

## 1. The Hunger Games

As far as famous books and their film adaptations go, [The Hunger Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hunger_Games) series has been criticized for the important details cut out, which inadvertently warped the history. To be specific, I know that I don't speak alone in saying that making the decision to strip away the meaning of the mockingjay pin and its origin by eradicating the background story behind how Katniss got it was very upsetting. In Suzanne Collins's novel, the pin is given to Katniss by her friend Madge Undersee, connecting Katniss to District 12's history. In the film, Katniss simply buys or receives it at the Hob. While I love The Hunger Games franchise (both the films and books as a whole), I feel that this move changed key emotional dynamics and simplified Katniss's motivation. ## 2. The Hunger Games: Catching Fire

While I loved the sequel, I have to admit that I wish Haymitch's back story was more developed in the movie franchise. While his depiction as a clever alcoholic fits in with the tune of the book, a large part of what makes him who he is has yet to be delved into. I really wanted to see how they would handle showing the inner workings of why he is the way he is—from his time in the Hunger Games arena to being the victor of the 50th Hunger Games (the Second Quarter Quell). In the novel, Katniss and Peeta watch the tape of Haymitch's games, learning how he outsmarted the Capitol using the arena's forcefield. Skipping this in the movie deprived viewers of understanding his deep trauma and brilliant strategic mind.

## 3. Divergent

While many may be more inclined to disagree, I felt that the director's choice to tone down the scene in which Al—who, after betraying her and teaming up with Peter and the rest of his crew—tries to kill heroine Tris was a sound one. In the [novel Divergent](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13335037-divergent), there were more serious and vile connotations about where the situation was headed during the assault near the chasm. The film adaptation handled the violence more subtly, which kept the movie PG-13 while still capturing Tris's sense of betrayal. Understanding these [key differences between books and movies](#1) highlights how directors adjust dark themes for wider theatrical audiences.

Key reasons filmmakers modify original book plots for cinema include:

- Time constraints and pacing considerations for feature-length runtime
- Rating requirements to ensure accessibility for broader audiences
- Translating internal character monologues into visual storytelling

## 4. The Lord of the Rings Series

While it's understandable that screen time has to be taken into consideration, it seemed as if there were more changes (characters as well as themes) made to the film than not. I don't think I can ever get over the fact that in the film, Glorfindel and Tom Bombadil were completely erased from the history of Tolkien's book. According to [The Lord of the Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings) lore, Glorfindel was the powerful elf lord who rescued Frodo and rode him to Rivendell, a role given to Arwen in Peter Jackson's film. Meanwhile, Tom Bombadil was an enigmatic figure who saved the hobbits in the Old Forest. While Arwen's expanded role enhanced her character arc, purists still miss these iconic literary figures.

## 5. The Mortal Instruments: City of Bones

The powerful Hodge's many tricks aren't as widely explored in the film. If you hadn't read the book before catching the film, you may not have known that one of the ways that Hodge always managed to be one step ahead of everyone was that he enlisted the help of his bird Hugo and his cat Church to spy on everyone. In Cassandra Clare's book, this magical surveillance added an intriguing layer of mystery and tension to the Institute that was unfortunately lost in the movie adaptation.

## 6. The Vampire Academy

I still wonder why the scene where Lissa witnesses blood donors being abused was left out. This was actually one of the moments in the book that helped paint her character in full. It's possible that the director wanted to avoid making the film as dark as the book and therefore sought to get rid of any instances that showcased her emotional issues. By omitting these harrowing moments, the movie lost some of the psychological depth that made Richelle Mead's novel so captivating to readers who value emotional complexity. ## 7. Ender's Game

Lastly, Ender's Game took a page out of the aforementioned film's book by completely erasing the lead character's penchant for violence and revenge. In the [novel Ender's Game](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/375802.Ender_s_Game) by Orson Scott Card, Ender Wiggin actually ends up killing Stilson and Bonzo after being bullied by them in extreme self-defense situations. In the film, this fatal outcome was softened to make Ender appear less cold and ruthless. Removing these lethal confrontations altered Ender's psychological burden and moral conflict.

That being said, one can say that all of the differences cataloged give the films a chance to exist as their own entities. It's up to you to decide whether they enhanced your experience or left you wishing for a faithful adaptation.
